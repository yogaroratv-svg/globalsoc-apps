DMARC explained, with a real domain

SPF, DKIM and DMARC decide whether a criminal can send email that looks like it came from you. Here's how they work, using our own domain, globalsoc.com.au, as the example, plus a lab you can run from your laptop.

DMARC in plain English

Email was designed in the 1980s with no way to prove who sent a message. Anyone can type any address into the From: line. That's why fake invoices and "urgent request from the CEO" emails work so well.

When an email arrives claiming to be from blah@globalsoc.com.au, the receiving server (Gmail, Outlook and so on) asks three questions. You answer each one by publishing a record in your domain's DNS:

CheckThe question it answersOur live record
SPFIs the server that sent this allowed to send email for globalsoc.com.au?v=spf1 include:_spf.mx.cloudflare.net include:_spf.google.com include:spf.protection.outlook.com -all
Cloudflare, Google and Microsoft may send. Everyone else is rejected (-all).
DKIMWas this email signed with globalsoc.com.au's private key, and is it unchanged since?selector1-azurecomm-prod-net._domainkey
Publishes the public key receivers use to check the signature.
DMARCDid SPF or DKIM pass for the same domain shown in From:? And if not, what should I do?v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:yogesh@globalsoc.com.au

The key idea: alignment

SPF and DKIM on their own don't stop spoofing. A scammer can send from their own server, at their own domain, and pass SPF for their domain, while the From: line your staff actually see says accounts@globalsoc.com.au.

DMARC adds one ruleThe domain that passed SPF or DKIM must match the domain in the visible From: address. That match is called alignment. If neither check passes with an aligned domain, DMARC fails, and the scammer's trick stops working.

Only one of the two needs to pass and align. In practice DKIM is the one to rely on, because the signature survives when email is forwarded and SPF often doesn't.

Policies and reports

The DMARC record tells receivers two things. First, the policy (p=) says what to do with email that fails:

p=noneDeliver it anyway, but report it to me. Where everyone starts, and where we are today.
p=quarantinePut it in the spam folder.
p=rejectBlock it outright. Full protection.

Second, the report address (rua=): every day, the big mailbox providers send you a summary of every server that sent email claiming to be your domain, and whether it passed. That's how you find the services you forgot about (your website's contact form, your accounting system, a newsletter tool) before you switch to p=reject and accidentally block them.

Why not jump straight to p=reject?Because the first reports almost always reveal a legitimate sender that isn't set up yet. A common example is replying from Gmail using "Send mail as" with your company address: the message is signed by gmail.com, not your domain, so it isn't aligned. Fix those first, then tighten the policy step by step.

Hands-on lab: send as blah@globalsoc.com.au

Here's exactly what we did on a Windows laptop. We send with Azure Communication Services (ACS), which needs a verified custom domain. Verifying it is the part that adds the SPF and DKIM records above. The commands use PowerShell and the Azure CLI. Replace the placeholders in <angle brackets> with your own resource names.

1

Look at the three records

Resolve-DnsName globalsoc.com.au -Type TXT | Select -Expand Strings          # SPF
Resolve-DnsName _dmarc.globalsoc.com.au -Type TXT | Select -Expand Strings   # DMARC
Resolve-DnsName selector1-azurecomm-prod-net._domainkey.globalsoc.com.au -Type CNAME   # DKIM → Azure's key

2

Create the sender "blah"

A verified ACS domain only sends from usernames you create, so nobody can quietly send as ceo@.

az communication email domain sender-username create `
  --email-service-name <email-service> -g <resource-group> `
  --domain-name globalsoc.com.au --sender-username blah --username blah --display-name "Blah Blah"
3

Send a test email

$cs = az communication list-key -n <communication-service> -g <resource-group> --query primaryConnectionString -o tsv
az communication email send --connection-string $cs `
  --sender "blah@globalsoc.com.au" --to "you@example.com" `
  --subject "DMARC test" --text "Hello from blah"
📸 Screenshot comingThe test email from blah@globalsoc.com.au landing in Yogesh's inbox
4

Read the verdict

In Gmail, open the email and choose ⋮ → Show original. The summary at the top is the receiving server's verdict:

SPF:   PASS with IP …
DKIM:  'PASS' with domain globalsoc.com.au
DMARC: 'PASS'

Scroll down to the Authentication-Results: header. You'll see header.d=globalsoc.com.au (who signed it) and header.from=globalsoc.com.au (what you saw). They match. That's alignment, and it's why DMARC passes.

📸 Screenshot comingGmail "Show original" with SPF, DKIM and DMARC all passing
5

Compare it with a non-aligned email

Now send yourself an email through a service that isn't set up for your domain, such as Gmail's "Send mail as" with your company address. Open "Show original" again. DKIM will be signed by gmail.com, which doesn't match the From: domain. With p=reject, that email could be blocked. This is exactly what DMARC reports warn you about.

6

Read your first DMARC report

Within a day or two, emails with zipped XML attachments arrive at the rua address. Unzip one and you'll find a row for each server that sent email as your domain:

<row>
  <source_ip>…</source_ip>  <count>3</count>
  <policy_evaluated><dkim>pass</dkim><spf>pass</spf></policy_evaluated>
</row>
<identifiers><header_from>globalsoc.com.au</header_from></identifiers>

Rows that fail show either a service you need to fix, or someone pretending to be you.
